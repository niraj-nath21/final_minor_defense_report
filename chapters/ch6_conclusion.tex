\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This project set out to determine whether NEAT can evolve a competent agent for
Super Mario Bros.\ on ordinary consumer hardware, without hand-crafted
heuristics and without the cost of a pixel-based deep reinforcement learning
pipeline. It can, and the objective stated in
Section~\ref{sec:objective} was met: the agent reads its state directly from
emulator RAM, reduces it to a $16 \times 13$ tile grid of 208 input features,
and trains entirely on the CPU. That reduction, roughly 295 times smaller than
the $256 \times 240$ raw frame, is what made every result in this report
reachable on a laptop rather than a cluster.

\noindent
The work was carried out as three training strategies run through one shared
pipeline, so that any difference in outcome is attributable to the training
strategy rather than to how the agent perceives the game. Each approach
answered a question and exposed the limitation that motivated the next.

\noindent
Approach I established the baseline. Training a separate population per level
solved Level 1-1 outright, with the population reaching the flagpole at
generation 500 and holding it for the remaining 600 generations, while Levels
3-1 and 2-1 reached 90.7\% and 75.3\% of their length respectively. The
structural traces confirm that NEAT complexified as intended: every run began
with zero hidden nodes and 1248 enabled connections, and ended with hidden
structure accumulated and the dense initial wiring pruned to between 100 and
320 connections. The agent did not simply grow; it discarded most of what it
started with and replaced a small part of it with useful internal structure.
The cost of this approach is that its genomes are specialists that do not
transfer, which reproduces the limitation reported for
MarI/O~\cite{sethbling2015}.

\noindent
Approach II removed that specialisation by requiring one genome to play several
levels before receiving a single combined score. Its champions carried around
150 hidden nodes against the 8 to 50 seen in level-specific runs, which is
consistent with a policy that must encode responses to situations arising on
only some of the levels. The species trace also showed the population
repeatedly converging onto a single strategy and having to be rebuilt by the
diversity reset mechanism, indicating that the multi-level objective is
substantially harder to satisfy than the single-level one.

\noindent
Approach III changed what a genome practises rather than what it is scored on,
evaluating from a library of 57 mid-level moments so that difficult sections
are trained as often as the opening stretch. This exposed a problem the
combined score of Approach II had concealed. Under a plain mean, consistency
across levels is not actually selected for: the champion scored 190.2 on Level
1-1 but only 88.5 on Level 2-1, a spread of 101.7, because a large gain on an
easy level fully compensates a large loss on a hard one. The spread penalty
introduced in this project, which subtracts a multiple of the cross-level
standard deviation from the mean, corrected this. It reduced the spread to 52.1
and cut the cross-level standard deviation from an average of 261 to 24 over
the shared evaluation window, a reduction of roughly eleven times. It did so by
trading peak performance on the easy levels for competence on the hard one,
raising Level 2-1 from 88.5 to 125.7 while Level 1-1 fell from 190.2 to 143.8,
which is precisely the behaviour it was designed to produce. The mean distance
covered per moment supports the same reading, rising from 163 to 177 under the
spread penalty while falling from 230 to 188 under the mean.

\noindent
The most useful finding of this project is not a score but a diagnosis. Nearly
every failure encountered here was located in the fitness function rather than
in the network or the evolutionary algorithm. The specialist that would not
transfer, the population that converged onto a single strategy, and the mean
that quietly traded away a level were all cases of the agent optimising exactly
what it had been asked to optimise. The corrections that resolved them were
changes to the question being asked, not to the learner answering it. For a
neuroevolutionary system in a domain of this kind, the design of the objective
is the substantive engineering problem, and the topology search will faithfully
exploit any weakness left in it.

\section{Recommendations}
\label{sec:recommendations}

The following recommendations follow from what this project observed and are
directed at anyone extending this work or applying NEAT to a comparable
control domain.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Treat the fitness function as the primary object of design.}
    Every substantive failure documented in this report originated there.
    Before attributing poor performance to the algorithm or to insufficient
    training, verify that the objective actually rewards the behaviour
    intended, since evolutionary search will exploit any discrepancy between
    the two.

    \item \textbf{Bound individual fitness terms whenever scores are
    aggregated.} An unbounded term on one level or one moment will dominate a
    mean and silently determine selection. The velocity gate, the capped jump
    reward and the proportional death penalty described in
    Section~\ref{subsec:approach-multilevel} were each introduced in response
    to an observed degenerate behaviour, and each was necessary.

    \item \textbf{Do not aggregate multi-task fitness with a plain mean.} The
    comparison in Section~\ref{sec:results-approach-3} shows a plain mean
    permitting an order-of-magnitude larger inconsistency across levels than a
    spread-penalised alternative. Where uniform competence is the goal, the
    aggregation must charge explicitly for variance.

    \item \textbf{Prefer a compact structured state representation to raw
    pixels where the environment exposes its state.} Reading RAM removed the
    need for a convolutional front end whose only function would have been to
    recover information the game already stores explicitly, and it is the
    single decision most responsible for this project running without a GPU.

    \item \textbf{Distribute evaluation across the state space rather than the
    episode start.} Starting every episode at the opening frame spends
    evolutionary pressure overwhelmingly on ground the population has already
    mastered. Moment-based evaluation addresses this directly and is
    recommended for any level-structured environment.

    \item \textbf{Log per-generation metrics and checkpoint champions
    separately from the population.} Runs here lasted hours and reached 4054
    generations. Every figure in Chapter~\ref{ch:result-discussion} was drawn
    from the CSV logs, and a run whose history is lost must be repeated in
    full.

    \item \textbf{Judge progress on behaviour, not on the score.} Fitness under
    one formulation is not comparable to fitness under another. Distance
    covered, network structure and cross-level consistency retained their
    meaning across all three approaches and were the only measures on which
    they could honestly be compared.
\end{itemize}

\section{Limitations and Future Work}
\label{sec:limitations-future-work}

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{subsec:limitations}

The results in this report should be read with the following constraints in
mind.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Limited training time and compute.} All training was confined
    to the CPU on consumer hardware, which bounded how far any run could be
    taken. Approach II was analysed over a window of 154 generations and
    Approach III over 161 and 87 generations, all considerably shorter than the
    level-specific runs. Several of the trends reported here are therefore
    established over shorter horizons than would be ideal.

    \item \textbf{Incomplete level coverage.} Only Level 1-1 was solved
    outright. Level 3-1 reached 90.7\% of its length and Level 2-1 stalled at
    75.3\% with no improvement over the final 200 generations of its run, so
    the agent does not complete the majority of the levels it was trained on.

    \item \textbf{The comparison in Approach III is not a paired run from
    scratch.} The spread penalty run covers 87 generations against the mean
    scheme's 161 and resumes from an existing checkpoint rather than starting
    fresh. The two are compared over the 87 generations they share. The
    magnitude of the difference makes the conclusion secure, but a matched pair
    of full runs would state it more firmly.

    \item \textbf{Single runs per configuration.} Each configuration was run
    once. Evolutionary algorithms are stochastic and outcomes vary between
    seeds, so no confidence interval can be placed on any figure reported here
    and the results should be read as single observations rather than as
    means over repeated trials.

    \item \textbf{The spread penalty does not constrain uniformly poor
    genomes.} A genome that performs equally badly everywhere has a standard
    deviation near zero and escapes the penalty. Early in a run the term is
    close to constant across the population and contributes little selection
    pressure, so evolution proceeds effectively on the mean until genuine
    differences emerge.

    \item \textbf{The moment dataset is small and scout-dependent.} The library
    holds 57 moments across four levels, unevenly distributed, with 21 for
    Level 1-1 but only 7 for Level 2-1. The moments are sampled by a scripted
    scout, so the dataset reflects the states that scout happened to reach and
    inherits whatever bias its policy carries.

    \item \textbf{Diminishing returns within a run.} By 20\% of each Approach I
    run, Level 1-1 had already improved 6.7-fold and Level 2-1 5.8-fold over
    their generation-0 distances, with the remaining 80\% contributing
    comparatively little. Level 3-1 ran nearly four times as long as Level 1-1
    and finished with a smaller relative gain, so additional generations alone
    do not reliably convert into proportionate improvement.

    \item \textbf{Restricted state representation.} The tile grid covers only
    the current screen and encodes four values per cell. The agent has no
    memory of what has passed off-screen, no lookahead beyond the viewport, and
    no access to powerup state, score or the timer. Velocity is read for
    scoring but never given to the network.

    \item \textbf{Narrow evaluation scope.} Work was limited to four levels of
    a single game. Generalisation to unseen levels was never measured on a
    held-out set, and no comparison against a DRL baseline such as DQN or PPO
    was implemented, so the efficiency argument for neuroevolution rests on
    the literature rather than on a controlled experiment conducted here.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Future Work}
\label{subsec:future-work}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Longer training runs on stronger hardware.} The most direct
    extension is to run each configuration for substantially more generations
    with a larger population. Level 2-1 and Level 3-1 both ended their runs
    short of completion, and Approaches II and III were analysed over windows
    of well under 200 generations. Parallelising evaluation across more cores,
    or across several machines, would allow the multi-level and moment-based
    approaches to be taken to the depth Approach I reached.

    \item \textbf{Systematic hyperparameter optimisation.} The NEAT
    configuration used here, including the population size of 150, the
    mutation rates, the speciation threshold and the survival proportion, was
    fixed early and not tuned. So was the spread penalty coefficient
    $\lambda$, which directly controls the trade between peak performance and
    consistency. A search over these values would establish how much of the
    remaining gap is a limitation of the method and how much is an untuned
    configuration.

    \item \textbf{Repeated runs with multiple seeds.} Running each
    configuration several times under different random seeds and reporting
    means with confidence intervals would replace the single observations in
    this report with statistically defensible claims.

    \item \textbf{A larger and better-balanced moment dataset.} Expanding
    beyond 57 moments and equalising coverage across levels would reduce the
    dependence on the scout's particular trajectory. Sampling moments from the
    trajectories of trained genomes as they improve, rather than from a fixed
    scripted scout, would keep the dataset aligned with the states the
    population actually encounters.

    \item \textbf{Adaptive and curriculum-based aggregation.} The spread
    penalty is currently applied with a fixed coefficient throughout. Given
    that it contributes little pressure early in a run, scheduling $\lambda$ to
    increase as the population improves would concentrate the consistency
    pressure where it is effective. Related to this, weighting moments by
    difficulty, or promoting harder moments as easier ones are mastered, would
    build an explicit curriculum.

    \item \textbf{Held-out generalisation testing.} Reserving levels that never
    appear in training and evaluating champions on them would measure
    generalisation directly, rather than inferring it from cross-level
    consistency on the training set.

    \item \textbf{A richer state representation.} Adding a limited history of
    recent frames, extending the grid beyond the current viewport, or including
    powerup state would give the network information it presently lacks.
    Whether the additional inputs justify the enlarged search space is an
    empirical question worth answering.

    \item \textbf{Comparison against a DRL baseline.} Implementing DQN or PPO
    on the same RAM-derived state representation would allow the efficiency
    claim motivating this project to be tested directly, comparing sample
    efficiency, wall-clock training time and final performance under identical
    conditions.

    \item \textbf{Novelty search and quality-diversity methods.} The plateaus
    visible in every Approach I run, most notably Level 3-1 sitting at roughly
    74\% from generation 1500 to generation 3000, are the characteristic
    signature of a population trapped in a local optimum. Novelty
    search~\cite{lehman2011} rewards behavioural difference rather than
    objective performance and is a natural candidate for escaping exactly this
    situation.

    \item \textbf{Extension to the full game and to other environments.}
    Scaling from four levels to the complete set of levels, and subsequently to
    other platforming titles, would test whether the approach generalises
    beyond the specific game studied here.
\end{itemize}
